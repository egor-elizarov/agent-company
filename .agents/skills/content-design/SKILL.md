# Website content design

## When to use

Use when the page goal, audience, and information hierarchy are known and the task is to write or revise visitor-facing website copy. This skill is for content decisions, not visual redesign or implementation.

## Inputs

- Current brief, product/UX analysis, visual or content-structure constraints, and confirmed user clarifications.
- Factual source material such as product documentation, project records, or approved case notes; existing copy and critique when revising.
- The requested content scope and intended reader action, if any. Treat source material as evidence, not text to reproduce wholesale.

## Process

1. **Map the decision journey.** Identify what the target visitor needs to recognize, understand, trust, and do. Write for their likely situations and decisions, rather than imposing a generic content structure.
2. **Build an evidence map.** Select facts that support the current journey and trace their provenance. Check dates, ownership, attribution, and the status of claimed work or capabilities. Where relevant, distinguish employment, client work, and exploration. Evidence for a claim does not itself grant permission to publish it.
3. **Set the content hierarchy.** Give related topics distinct purposes and scopes. Make the main topic more prominent when evidence supports it. State supported facts confidently; locate uncertainty in unconfirmed claims or developing capabilities instead of hedging every sentence.
4. **Draft concise page copy.** Use headings and supporting content that add relevant context, evidence, or a next step. Make abstract statements tangible through a selected, verified context. Select useful facts rather than reproducing source documents wholesale. Avoid generic claims, repeated framing, jargon without purpose, and promises the sources do not support.
5. **Fit the intended design.** Check heading length, paragraph density, mobile scanning order, and any action labels against the visual structure using the actual draft. Where actions are present, keep their hierarchy and labels clear and consistent; do not invent destinations or commitments.
6. **Audit and revise.** Record claim sources, attribution, and publication boundaries separately from any outstanding factual confirmations. Reuse applicable recorded publication approval. When given critique, assess each finding against the goal and evidence; change what improves the copy and explain consequential recommendations not adopted.

## Expected output

Provide proposed final copy in the requested location and page order, including the relevant headline, supporting text, section content, actions, and metadata. Add brief source notes for factual claims and only publication checks that materially matter. For a revision, include a short account of important changes and reasoned decisions to preserve existing copy.

## Quality checks

- A visitor can identify the page's relevance, credible basis, and intended next step, where applicable, without decoding broad labels.
- Every factual claim has a source and respects its publication boundaries.
- Attribution preserves who did the work and its status; for example, employment results are not client outcomes, and exploration is not established expertise.
- Related topics serve distinct reader needs; confidence matches the evidence for each.
- The copy fits the intended visual structure, is concise and natural, and avoids unnecessary hedging, repetition, and generic promotional language.
- Any remaining factual or publication confirmation is specific and necessary.
