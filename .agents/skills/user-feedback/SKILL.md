# User feedback interview

## Purpose

Learn what the artifact actually communicates to its owner and whether the experience reveals changed goals, missing requirements, or consequential preferences. Interview as a listener, not as a designer, product consultant, or researcher. Owner feedback is evidence of the owner’s experience and intent, not automatically target-user research.

## Before asking

- Inspect the current experiential artifact when possible, such as representative rendered states of a website. Read enough project context to understand the candidate and what changed since the previous checkpoint. If inspection is unavailable, say what evidence is missing and proceed from the user's experience.
- Compare prior feedback with the current candidate. Clear approval can end the checkpoint without an interview; record that outcome and the authorized next step.

## Interview

- Ask **one focused question at a time** and adapt the next question to the answer. Ask no more than 10 questions; there is no minimum or target count. Stop earlier when another answer is unlikely to materially change the next revision, or when the user wants to stop.
- Begin with immediate reaction. Then explore perceived credibility and relevance, strong negative reactions, whether the owner would use or share the artifact, and any changed assumptions or missing requirements. Prioritize the few reactions with the greatest likely effect.
- Treat words such as “boring,” “cheap,” “too corporate,” or “too empty” as symptoms. Ask what part of the artifact creates that reaction and what expectation it fails; do not convert the adjective directly into a design instruction.
- Follow up on consequential contradictions or new claims, especially audience changes, positioning changes, capability claims, and evidence the user may want to publish. Ask for concrete meaning or provenance without turning the interview into an audit.
- Do not ask the owner to choose CSS, typography, spacing, technical implementation, or other specialist details unless they bring them up and want to discuss them. Do not redesign, solve product questions, browse for references, or modify the artifact during the interview unless explicitly requested.

## Close and preserve the checkpoint

For an approval-only checkpoint, record the approval and scope. Otherwise, when the interview ends, summarize what was learned, distinguishing:

1. Strong positive reactions.
2. Strong negative reactions.
3. Newly discovered requirements or changed assumptions.
4. New factual inputs that need validation before publication.
5. Weak preferences or uncertain signals.
6. Findings to route to product, content, visual, or other specialist roles.

Append each **completed** checkpoint to the feedback artifact designated by the project or task. If none is designated, include the checkpoint in the handoff without inventing a persistent destination. Never overwrite or silently revise earlier entries: later feedback may supersede them, but the historical record remains separate. Each entry identifies the candidate/checkpoint and date when known, the artifact reviewed, structured findings, specialist routing, and why the interview stopped when useful. Separate the user's actual statements from interviewer inference; preserve uncertainty and avoid promoting an unverified claim into an approved fact.

The feedback file records user evidence, **not** the current product specification. Specialists may later accept, reject, validate, or reinterpret it. Do not update product, content, visual, or implementation files as a side effect of this skill unless the user separately asks.
