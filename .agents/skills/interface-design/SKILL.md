# Web/interface design direction

## When to use

Use for web/interface design after a product brief and UX direction define the audience, goal, primary journey, content priorities, and scope. Create or revise a visual direction; do not implement the interface unless separately requested.

## Inputs

- Current brief, product/UX analysis, constraints, and confirmed decisions.
- Existing visual direction and critique, when revising.
- Representative content: approved wording where available, otherwise clearly marked layout probes based only on the brief.

## Process

1. **Trace the product hierarchy.** Identify what users must notice, understand, trust, and do. Preserve the relative prominence of established facts, hypotheses, and emerging ideas.
2. **Derive one visual principle.** Connect a distinctive product or positioning idea to a repeatable choice in composition, typography, or information treatment. Explain the connection. Avoid a mood label that could fit any site or decoration that implies unsupported claims.
3. **Resolve hierarchy with content.** Set the order and relative weight of the opening, major sections, evidence, and primary action. Test short and long representative wording at constrained heights; specify what yields when space is limited.
4. **Choose a coherent system.** Give a reason for typography, color roles, layout, spacing, imagery, interaction, and motion. Make concrete choices where evidence permits. Treat exact font sizes, hex values, and dimensions as working values until checked with actual content and states; avoid precision that hides an unresolved design decision.
5. **Define behavior across available space.** Explain when columns stack, how reading and focus order persist, and where actions remain findable. Select representative checks appropriate to the stage. For an early candidate, inspect key narrow and wide states; identify relevant intermediate widths, short viewports, and resized text for later hardening. Prefer content-fit rules over arbitrary device breakpoints.
6. **Set accessibility checks.** Specify contrast, visible focus, link identification, target size and spacing, reflow, readable zoom, reduced motion, and treatment of meaningful versus decorative images. Use the applicable current standard. Separate proposed acceptance criteria from checks actually performed; validate states appropriate to the current stage and record remaining checks.
7. **Use research selectively.** When references would clarify a visual decision, inspect current real sites and authoritative design sources. Record the observable mechanism, the decision it informs, and what should not transfer. Distinguish visual evidence from a site that merely demonstrates good copy or content structure. Do not copy assets, claims, or a complete style.

## Expected output

Write a concise design direction at the requested location. Include the visual principle and rationale, hierarchy, typography, color, layout and spacing, major content treatments, responsive behavior, interaction and motion, accessibility acceptance checks, and specific reference takeaways when research was useful. For a revision, summarize consequential changes and reasoned rejections of critique.

## Quality checks

- A reader can trace each major visual decision to a user need, product priority, constraint, or observed reference.
- The primary journey and action remain clear at constrained widths and heights; emerging or uncertain content cannot be mistaken for established proof.
- Representative content exposes likely wrapping, density, and scanning problems without being passed off as approved copy.
- The direction is distinctive enough to guide a designer, yet flexible enough to survive real content and responsive layouts.
- Accessibility criteria are testable; reported results identify the states actually checked. The output contains no implementation code.
